The briefing

Britain, on display.

Diplomacy, defence, trade, science, and sixteen days of Kensington Palace finding out just how effectively a handsome unmarried prince can be sold to the American public.

The visit has been under discussion since 2016, but the referendum gives it urgency. The government wants senior contact in Washington, reassurance for investors and defence partners, and a programme that shows Britain's American relationships go deeper than whichever ministers happen to be in office.

Donald Trump is pleased to receive the Prince of Wales. He likes royal association, ceremony and the attention attached to both. London will provide the meeting and the photographs, but does not want the visit read as a personal endorsement. So Washington also gets Congress, Arlington, Walter Reed and the Smithsonian, and most of the tour happens outside the capital.

More than four hundred journalists apply for accreditation. Most of them are American.

He is thirty-one, handsome, unmarried, a decorated veteran, and already followed by an American press that can sell photographs of him whether the subject is wounded servicemen, spacecraft or polo. The White House wants that. So does every television producer, city official, charity and museum fighting for a slot.

For Kensington Palace it's also the first major tour run substantially by Alexander's own office. Marianne treats it as a chance to sell him as the future of the monarchy: young, independent, military, educated, attractive, and visibly unlike the institution behind him. She brings in Clara Bell, a stylist used to dressing actors. By the second fitting he's offering opinions and checking how the clothes sit from several angles.

None of this is imposed on a reluctant prince. He's vain, he enjoys clothes, and he enjoys being wanted. The Warrior Games are the one thing he insists on. Johnson and JPL he welcomes for less elevated reasons: he grew up watching shuttle launches and thinks Mars rovers are extremely cool.

Whose tour is it?

Canada was a realm, so Canada ran it and paid. The United States is a foreign country, so this is a British visit abroad. The Foreign Office asks for it, the Embassy and four consulates build each leg, and the British government pays for the travel.

He isn't a head of state, so the Secret Service doesn't protect him. The State Department's Diplomatic Security Service does, with his own Met officers inside their detail.

MON 26 JUN · 07.15 · THE STUDY, BRITISH RESIDENCE

Four hours' sleep, and the study smelled of furniture polish and somebody else's grandmother. The Embassy had put coffee on in a silver pot the size of a fire extinguisher. It was terrible. He drank it anyway, standing at the window with no jacket on yet, watching a sprinkler tick across the lawn like it was being paid by the hour.

Marianne had the programme printed. She always did. She trusted paper, a stapler, and very little else.

"Four minutes of pool at the top. Then twenty-five without cameras. You're out before the Indians arrive."

"What does he want?"

"To be seen with you."

"And what do we want?"

"For you to be seen with him and for nothing else to happen." She turned the page. "He'll mention your mother. He'll mention your looks. Don't answer either."

Charlie, on the window seat behind three newspapers: "He might do Scotland. The golf courses."

"Christ."

"Just say Turnberry's beautiful."

"Turnberry is beautiful. That's the fucking problem."

Nobody laughed. Marianne had the pen behind her ear, which only came out after the second coffee. He read the card. Mother: no. Looks: no. Golf: Turnberry. Four minutes. He could do four minutes standing on his head. He'd done Walter Reed yesterday with a Marine who'd lost both hands and wanted to talk about Mourinho, and he'd been fine. Better than fine. He'd been exactly the right amount of everything.

Clara had left a tie on the bed. Navy with a red stripe, which was clever of her, because it was also very nearly the President's colours and nobody could say he'd made a point.

In the car he counted flags on the embassies going up Mass Ave. Brazil. Turkey. Something green he didn't recognise. Miranda said two minutes, sir. He did up his jacket.

"He didn't do the sad face. He asked how long I'd had the leg and then we talked soccer for like ten minutes."

MON 26 JUN · 10.06 · THE OVAL OFFICE

The carpet was the first thing. Gold, with the eagle woven into the middle of it, and up close it looked like something out of a casino in Leeds. He fixed his eyes about a foot to the left of the eagle's head and kept them there.

"This guy. This guy is a hero. A real hero, not like some of them, believe me. He flew helicopters in Afghanistan, very dangerous helicopters, and he saved Americans. A lot of Americans. Is that right? I was told a lot."

"Some, sir. It was a joint—"

"A lot of Americans. People don't know that. The fake news won't tell you that. And he got hurt, very badly hurt, you can see—" a finger, wagging somewhere near the scar, "—and he's not complaining. He doesn't complain. Tremendous courage. His mother would be very proud. Beautiful woman. Very sad, very sad what happened. And he's popular, very popular, the American people are going to love him, they already love him, have you seen the coverage? Unbelievable coverage. Better than mine, almost." Laughter from the pool. "Almost."

A hand landed on his shoulder and stayed. It patted twice, the way you'd pat a horse you'd just bought.

Behind the desk the windows were tall and old-fashioned, with the Rose Garden green in the bottom panes, and he knew from someone who'd been briefed by someone that the glass was about three inches thick. He measured it anyway. Two strides from where he was standing. Head first, a bit of a run-up. Charlie could brief it as a medical episode.

He smiled at the eagle. He'd been smiling at the eagle for about two minutes now and his face had set like that, a sort of pleasant, interested, slightly glazed expression, the one he used for school prize-givings and people's holiday photos. He had no idea what else to do with it. There was a protocol for being insulted by a head of state. There was a protocol for being ignored by one. Nobody had ever written one for being complimented to death on camera by one, and he was making it up as he went along: don't nod, it looks like agreement. Don't look at the pool. Don't look at Marianne. Don't do anything at all with your eyebrows.

"Do women still propose to you? In the crowds?"

"Not often, sir."

"They will here." To the room, delighted: "He's got the whole package. Look at him."

Twenty lenses looked at him. He could feel the lights on the back of his neck, and a bead of sweat setting off down his spine under the shirt, slowly, like it had all the time in the world.

At the back somebody shouted about women, whether all the attention bothered him, and the President laughed and leant in and said it again, slower, for anyone who'd missed it. So handsome.

Oh, fuck off.

He turned his face away so it wouldn't show. It didn't help. His eyes went wide, his eyebrows went up a fraction, the pure disbelieving are you fucking serious of it, and then they rolled, all the way up and round, on their own, the way they'd gone at Caroline across the breakfast table since he was nine.

Oh. Oh, no.

one second

The pool had it. He heard them get it: twenty people breathing in at once, the shutters going like rain on a tin roof, then the shouting. Was he embarrassed. Did he like the President. Was Georgiana watching. He turned back with his expression put away and said thank you, Mr President, and that the Rose Garden was looking very well this year, and somebody from the press office was already herding them out.

The President thought it had gone brilliantly. He said so twice.

In the car Marianne said nothing until the Naval Observatory. Then she turned and looked at him for a long time without blinking, the way his mother used to look at him in church.

He looked out of the window. Brazil. Turkey. The green one.

TUE 27 JUN · 19.40 · SEVEN STOREYS DOWN

They'd cleared the place, and seven storeys under Manhattan it was cold, and his shoes were too loud. Leather soles. Clara's choice. He'd have worn something quieter if anyone had told him.

The curator called him Your Royal Highness at the lift, sir at the bottom of it, and sir at the start of every sentence after that, sometimes twice. At the Survivors' Stairs he told her, as kindly as he knew how, that she really didn't have to. She said "Sorry, sir," went pink to the ears, and managed three sentences without it.

Foundation Hall was enormous. The slurry wall ran up one side, rough and dark and wet-looking, and the Last Column stood in the middle covered in marker pen and spray paint, company numbers, unit badges, names. Then Ladder 3. The front of the cab was gone. Not crushed. Peeled, the metal curled back on itself like the lid of a sardine tin, so you could see the dashboard and the seats and a clipboard still wedged by the window.

He made himself look at it. He knew how to look at wreckage and work out what had happened to the people inside it. He'd have given a great deal not to know.

The director said quietly that there was a little further to go.

The gallery of faces was worse because it was so ordinary. Passport photos. Wedding photos. A man holding up a fish. She stopped every so often and pointed, and didn't need to look for them. Birmingham. A bank, the 104th floor. In for one meeting, her first time in New York. Sixty-seven.

Arlington on Sunday, Walter Reed after it, and he'd been fine. In the lift going up he couldn't get a breath all the way down, so he counted floors. B6. B5. B4. Marianne didn't say anything. She stood a little closer than usual, that was all.

East 51st Street took eleven minutes. Nobody spoke. At the Consul-General's door he did up his jacket, found his face, and walked into a room full of bankers holding champagne.

WED 5 JUL · 08.40 · NEUTRAL BUOYANCY LAB, SONNY CARTER

It was a hangar with a swimming pool in it. Forty feet deep, the size of a football pitch, and sitting on the bottom was a piece of the International Space Station. Not a model. The real dimensions, the real handrails, white and gold under six million gallons of water lit up like a lido.

Two astronauts in white suits hung down there with safety divers round them like pilot fish, working a bolt on a truss. Everything at a third of the speed. Six-hour sessions, the training lead said, every bolt and tether and handrail on the real walk, until they could do it in their sleep.

Six hours. He'd done long sorties and come home unable to feel his arse. He couldn't imagine it in a suit.

"Can I see the gloves?"

They brought him a spare. He worked his hand in and tried to make a fist and couldn't get it shut. That was unpressurised, she said. In vacuum it was worse. People lost fingernails.

"Fuck off," he said, delighted. "Sorry."

The Mars Yard was a patch of red dirt and rocks about the size of a tennis court behind a building in Pasadena, and it was the best place he had ever been.

"It decides that on its own?"

"Some of it. We tell it where to go. It works out how not to die on the way."

"Say that again. Slower."

She said it again, slower, crouched in the grit beside him with her sunglasses pushed up into grey hair. He understood about two-thirds and nodded through the rest, and she could tell, and didn't mind. Then she handed him a wheel. Aluminium, light as a biscuit tin, with a ragged hole torn through the tread.

"Same thing's happening to Curiosity. Sharp rocks. We're learning to drive round them."

He laughed out loud. He couldn't help it. He was crouched in the dirt in his good trousers holding a wheel whose twin was a hundred and forty million miles away, and a woman in Pasadena was steering it round rocks on a Thursday.
